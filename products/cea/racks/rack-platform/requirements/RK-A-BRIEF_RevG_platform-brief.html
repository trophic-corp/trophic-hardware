Decisions taken, Rev B

Four decisions returned on 04 Sep 2026. Two close out conflicts; two are instructions to research further before deciding. Nothing here was decided on your behalf.

D1

Tier architecture — resolved

Closed

Your decision: the main rack is structurally 5-tier capable and configurable down to 4, with 4 tiers as the recommended default and the 5th as an optional extension. Germination is a separate, more compact rack — 6 to 12 tiers, no lighting, no water, no sensors. High-volume germination is optional and must not constrain the main rack design.

What this fixes in the model

Number_of_Tiers = 4 default, range 3–5. Uprights are cut and drilled for 5 positions in every build, so the 5th tier is a bolt-on kit rather than a different SKU — the marginal cost of a longer upright is a few hundred rupees against the cost of stocking two upright lengths. Rack_Height is then driven by 5 × Shelf_Pitch plus base and headroom, with the 4-tier default leaving the top bed at a working height rather than a reaching height.

The germination rack becomes Product 1.1-G: the same upright and beam sections and the same joint, with a tighter pitch, a plain drained tray shelf, no LED rail, no duct interface, and a higher stacked load case. It shares the part standardisation matrix but has its own load path, and — per your instruction — it does not get a vote on the main rack's geometry. Where the two disagree, the growing rack wins.

D2

Irrigation — confirmed in principle, conditional on §9

Conditional

Your instruction: look into flood-and-drain properly; if it is feasible without impacting crop quality, design the irrigation to be interchangeable with other methods.

Finding — see §8 for the evidence

§1 · Engineering brief

Items A through U

What the five documents actually establish, and — more importantly — what they do not. Six of the twenty-one items are effectively silent on the rack.

A

Intended crops

DocumentedHigh

Primary: microgreens (Phase A Zone A). Secondary in the same facility: emersed aquatic/aquascaping plant propagation (Zone B, >80% RH) and saffron corms (Zone C, programmable two-phase temperature). Named future: leafy greens, herbs, medicinal plants, tissue-culture hardening — Phase A §2.3 states these are "simply a fourth or fifth zone profile added to the same control architecture."

Engineering implication: the microgreen configuration is the design case. Zone B maps cleanly onto a propagation variant of the same rack (higher RH, lower PPFD, humidity enclosure). Zone C does not — saffron corms need a substrate bed of order 150–200 mm and an insulated sub-chamber, which is a different shelf module and probably a different product. Confirm scope — Q19

B

Tray standards

Not in documentsLow

No document names a tray size. Phase A and Phase C specify "NFT trays/channels"; Phase D specifies SS304 sheet 0.8–1.2 mm for food-contact trays and injection-moulded NFT trays "per product design". The 1020 tray comes from your brief, not from Phase A–E.

External check: "1020" is a name, not a dimension. Measured outer dimensions are 536 × 279 × 64 mm (21.1 × 11.0 × 2.5 in, Bootstrap Farmer heavy-duty), inner bottom 505 × 239 mm, wall 1.8–2.0 mm, mass 0.52 kg. Johnny's trays are 533 × 279 mm. This single dimension propagates into shelf width, beam length, LED bar length, flood depth and rack footprint. Indian-supplied 1020 trays are not guaranteed to match US ones. I will not assume a value — Q1

C

Production methodology

DocumentedConflicts with your preference

Phase A §2.4 and Phase C Product 1.3 both specify NFT (nutrient film technique) for microgreens. You have stated a preference for flood-and-drain. Penn State Extension supports you: "sub-irrigation, such as with ebb-and-flow systems, works well for watering microgreens trays… helps keep leaves and stems dry, which is important to avoid disease."

My assessment is that the documents are wrong here and you are right — see Conflict 2. NFT is engineered for a persistent root mass sitting in a channel; microgreens are a media-grown, 7–21 day, harvest-the-whole-tray crop. This is a genuine correction to Phase A/C, not a preference.

Not in documentsLow

Production cycle

ExternalMed

The cycle contains a blackout germination stage that none of the five documents mentions — see item L. That stage has entirely different rack requirements (no light, stacked trays, weighted) and is the strongest argument for a separate germination configuration rather than dark-cycling a lit tier.

F

Lighting requirements

DocumentedHigh

PPFD working band 150–210 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹ (Phase A §1.1, restated Phase C 1.2). Below ~100 suppresses biomass and phytochemicals; above ~210 with far-red or UV-A tends to induce photoinhibition. Photoperiod 16–24 h; continuous 24 h raised both yield and phytochemical content in four Brassicaceae genotypes. Spectrum blue-enriched R:B, optionally +W/FR, 2–4 dimmable channels. Target diode efficacy 2.8–3.1 µmol/J (Phase D).

Close-canopy mounting is a stated design strategy, not an option (Phase A §1.5, Sheibani & Mitchell 2023): reducing fixture-to-canopy distance while dimming to hold PPFD constant cuts both lighting energy and HVAC load. This makes LED height adjustability a functional requirement, not a convenience.

An industry source gives 200–300 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹ — see Conflict 4. Not stated anywhere: fixture length, width, mass, mounting hole pattern, or whether the driver is onboard or remote. Q5

G

Temperature requirements

DocumentedHigh

Zone A microgreens 20–24 °C. Zone B not specified numerically. Zone C saffron 23–30 °C warm phase → 15–16 °C induction. Ooty ambient 5–25 °C; Phase A §2.1 notes the dominant HVAC problem is gentle heating and VPD management, not refrigeration.

Implication for the rack: thermal expansion over a 5–30 °C range is negligible for a 2 m frame in any candidate material (<1 mm) and is not a design driver. Temperature matters to the rack only through LED junction temperature and through condensation risk in the Zone B configuration.

H

Humidity requirements

PartialMed

Phase A specifies VPD-based control rather than raw RH, and gives >80% RH for Zone B aquatics with a humidity dome/misting subsystem. For Zone A microgreens it says only "moderate VPD" — no numeric RH or VPD target is given. External: germination 80–90% RH, growing 50–70%, pre-harvest 40–50%.

This matters more than it looks. A rack that must survive sustained >80% RH and misting in the propagation configuration rules out powder-coated mild steel and makes finish integrity — not bulk strength — the governing corrosion question. Q16

I

Airflow requirements

DocumentedHigh

Canopy-level horizontal velocity 0.2–0.5 m/s, upper practical bound ~1.3 m/s before mechanical/desiccation stress (Phase A §1.4, multiple validated CFD studies). Crucially: distributing air inlets along the full length of a growing shelf rather than from one end reduced the velocity coefficient of variation from ~33% to ~10%. A CFD-optimised multi-duct design reached 0.42 m/s at 44% CV.

This is the most design-determining horticultural specification in the entire document set, and it invalidates the common benchmark arrangement of one clip-on or 120 mm axial fan per shelf blowing along the shelf. The rack needs a distributed-inlet interface — a perforated duct, a plenum edge, or a linear fan array — as a first-class structural feature. Not stated: whether air is supplied centrally by room HVAC or generated per rack. Q6, Q13

C1

Frame material: the documents chose aluminium T-slot; your brief points at stainless

Architecture
Phase A T1, Phase B T1, Phase C 1.1, Phase E"80/20-style aluminium extrusion racks, tool-less reconfiguration" — already selected as Product 1.1, TRL 8–9, complexity Low, Year 1.
Your briefLists stainless-steel fabrication and sheet-metal design as a core competency, asks Phase 4 to compare SS304 / SS316 / galvanised / powder-coated steel / aluminium, and specifies a food-production environment.
Recommendation

Neither side has noticed the real problem: an open T-slot is a poor hygienic profile. The slots run the full length of every member, they trap growing media, seed hulls and water, they cannot be wiped clean, and they sit directly under a flooded tray bed. For a dry assembly frame that is irrelevant; for food production it is the first thing an auditor will point at. Phase B's own argument for extrusion is explicitly about visible premium cues and reconfigurability, not hygiene — and Phase D independently specifies SS304 for anything food-contact.

My recommendation is a hybrid rather than a winner: SS304 hollow-section uprights and beams carrying the wet, food-contact, cleanable zone; a bolt-on accessory rail — extrusion or slotted SS — carrying only the dry services above the canopy. That keeps the modularity Phase B is selling, keeps the wet structure wipe-clean, and keeps the aesthetic. But it is more expensive than pure extrusion, and it contradicts a product definition already committed in Phase C/E. This is genuinely yours to decide, and Q15/Q16 are the inputs I need.

Recommendation — I side with you against the documents

Microgreens are sown densely into a media tray, never develop a channel-compatible root structure, and are cut whole at 7–21 days. Phase C's own text concedes the NFT product "exists mainly to complete the catalogue and support the rack system, not as a standalone differentiator" and calls the segment "crowded" at 25–35% margin — the weakest product in the portfolio.

Flood-and-drain is the right primary method, and it should drain to waste or to a treated recovery loop, not recirculate directly. Your brief already warns against assuming recirculation; the reason is specific — in a dense microgreen canopy on a short cycle, recirculated solution is an efficient way to move Pythium and enteric contamination from one tray to every tray, and there is no long crop cycle over which to amortise UV or filtration hardware.

The modular answer to your question about accommodating more methods: make the tier a defined bay with a standard interface — flat, level, drained, with a supply port and a drain port at fixed positions. A flood table drops into that bay. So does a plain drain-to-waste tray shelf, a capillary matting shelf, a deep substrate bed for saffron corms, or an NFT channel set if a customer wants one. The rack should not know which method it is running.

The one caveat, and why it does not apply here

The classic objection to sub-irrigation is salt accumulation at the substrate surface: water moves upward by capillarity and evaporates, leaving salts behind, with no downward leaching fraction to flush them. In long-cycle container crops this is a genuine problem that forces periodic overhead leaching.

It is largely moot for microgreens. The crop is harvested at 7–21 days, the media is replaced every cycle rather than carried over, and fertilisation is light or absent until after germination. Salts do not have time to accumulate to a damaging concentration before the tray is stripped and reset. This is worth knowing precisely because it is the objection someone will raise — and the answer is that the crop cycle solves it.

The finding

Aluminium T-slot is not the cheap option. At Indian rates it lands within about 7% of SS304 and slightly above the SS304 hybrid — and it does so for two reasons that Phase C and Phase E do not account for.

First, extrusion carries a large conversion premium over commodity tube. Stainless is cheaper per metre than aluminium T-slot in this market. Second — and this is the cost nobody budgets for — a T-slot frame has roughly forty joints, and every one of them needs corner brackets, T-nuts and bolts. At ₹120–180 per joint that is ₹5,000–7,000 of connectors on a single rack, which quietly consumes the entire profile saving.

The result: among the three premium options, the spread is under ₹2,000 on a frame of roughly ₹22,000. Cost is not the deciding variable. §11 · Cost-down architecture

Stop buying one material to do three jobs

You are right that ₹22,000 on a frame alone is not defensible. But the answer is not a cheaper stainless — it is noticing that the frame was being asked to satisfy a requirement it never actually has.

The mistake in Rev B and C

Both the hybrid and the all-stainless option were specified to be food-contact grade throughout. That was wrong. Three different jobs were being bundled into one material, and only one of them needs stainless:

Structure

Carries load, resists impact, holds the beds level. Sees humidity and occasional splash. Never touches the crop or standing water. Needs corrosion protection, not food-grade metallurgy.

Wet zone

Contains the flood, touches the crop. This is the only part that must be food-grade — and it is per-tier, replaceable, and optional depending on irrigation method.

Accessory plane

Carries LEDs, sensors, cabling, future modules. Needs to be repositionable and extensible. Needs a defined interface, not a particular alloy.

Separating these is not a compromise, it is what the industry already does. US commercial greenhouse benching uses heavy-gauge galvanised steel legs with hot-dip galvanised expanded-metal decks, with tube ends capped "to seal out moisture and chemicals" — not stainless. Stainless appears in horticulture on food-contact surfaces, not on bench structure. Rev C had the boundary in the wrong place.

Stated plainly, because these are real and you should decide with them in view rather than discover them later.

Four levers, all available now

L1 · Delete the per-tier cross beams — ₹562

Rev D gave every tier a full rectangular frame: two long beams plus two short cross beams. But the deck already has its own edge frame, and its side members run front-to-back — so the deck is the cross tie. Building a second one in steel underneath it was duplication. Each tier drops to two long beams; the base keeps a full rectangle, and two flat rear diagonals handle racking in the depth plane. Beam length falls from 21.6 m to 15.4 m and frame mass from 45.1 to 39.6 kg.

This one is NEEDS VALIDATION, not PASS. Removing cross members and relying on the decks plus two diagonals for depth-plane stability is sound in principle — it is a braced box — but I have not run the frame stability check, and I am not going to claim it is safe on reasoning alone. It goes into Phase 17 as an explicit item. If it fails, the cross beams come back at ₹562 and nothing else in this section changes.

L2 · Make the finish a choice rather than a default — ₹2,700

The single biggest line, and the one worth thinking hardest about. Powder coating over galvanising is a duplex system — genuinely excellent, and genuinely optional. Commercial greenhouse benching runs on mill-finish hot-dip galvanised steel with no topcoat at all, in wetter conditions than your room will ever see. The zinc is the corrosion protection; the powder is appearance and a smoother wipe-down surface.

So: mill GI on Core, powder coat as a ₹2,700 option on Grow and Pro. That puts the money where the customer is paying for finish and takes it out of the rung that has to survive a price comparison. It is a positioning decision as much as a cost one, and it is yours — but note that hiding a ₹2,700 finish inside every rack was costing you the entry price point.

L3 · Twelve L-brackets instead of twenty plates — ₹560

Rev D used a pair of laser-cut plates per beam-to-upright joint. A single formed L-bracket does the same job with half the parts, half the picking and half the bolt count.

L4 · Correct the hardware and labour rates — ₹380

M12 levelling feet are ₹80–120 each in India, not the ₹150 I carried; caps are ₹15. With fewer parts to assemble, labour drops from ₹1,200 to ₹1,000.

C · What the X-brace actually does

It is doing a specific job created by a specific decision. In §12 I deleted the per-tier cross beams to save ₹562, on the grounds that the decks tie front to rear. That left the rack with no diagonal anywhere, so the load path now is:

  • Side-sway (rack leaning left–right): resisted by the rear X-brace. This is its job. The front plane deliberately has no diagonal — it must stay open for tray access — so the rear brace carries that plane for both.
  • Front–back leaning: resisted by the decks acting as horizontal diaphragms plus the base cross beams. This is the NEEDS VALIDATION item from §12, still open.

So it is not optional in the current design — it is the only diagonal in the structure, and it costs 2.07 kg of flat bar, roughly ₹210. Four ways to go:

D · The white enclosure — yes, and it does three jobs at once

Your instinct is right, and the airflow benefit is probably larger than the light one.

Light

Phase A §1.5 already establishes the principle: photons that strike walls, aisles and empty positions are pure waste, and capturing them is why close-canopy lighting saves energy. The geometry here, as an engineering estimate rather than a cited figure:

Edge spill, two bars at 144 mm above canopy, 120° beam — ENGINEERING ESTIMATE
Half-spread each side, 144 × tan 60°249 mm
Bar footprint across the bed depth≈ 498 mm
Flux falling outside the 560 mm bed≈ 14%
Plus end losses along the bar≈ 3–5%
Recovered by matte white at 85–90% reflectance≈ 8–12% more on canopy

Treat that as a range to verify, not a promise — measure it with the portable PAR sensor from §7 at commissioning, which is exactly what that instrument is for. The more valuable effect is not the average but the uniformity: the outer trays in a four-across bed currently receive less light than the inner two, and edge reflectors close that gap. Uniformity is what decides whether all four trays crop evenly enough to harvest on the same day.

Use matte white, not mylar or foil. Specular surfaces create bright spots and make uniformity worse; diffuse white scatters. This is consistent across the horticultural lighting guidance.

Airflow — the stronger argument

This is what §7 Q13.3 was asking about. An open rack lets plenum air short-circuit around the canopy rather than through it. Side and rear panels turn each tier into a duct, which is what makes the 0.2–0.5 m/s canopy velocity and the low coefficient of variation from Phase A §1.4 actually achievable rather than aspirational. If you are building the distributed plenum at all, the panels are what make it work.

Structure — but do not use it

A rear panel is an excellent shear diaphragm, far stiffer than a diagonal. Resist the temptation. Keep it non-structural so it comes off for cleaning without compromising the frame.

E · Plastic moulding in the family — worth pursuing, with one caution

This could change the flood tray economics, but the process matters more than the access. For a 1176 × 560 × 47 mm tray:

  • Thermoforming / vacuum forming is the right process — tooling is a single aluminium or resin form, typically ₹30,000–80,000 at this size, and it would take the tray from about ₹1,228 fabricated to well under ₹700.
  • Injection moulding is not viable at this size for a startup — a 1176 mm part needs a very large press and a tool costing several lakh. If his shop is injection, the tray is the wrong part to bring him.
  • What is worth bringing him either way: the 1020 trays themselves, sensor and junction enclosures, cable clips, tube end caps, LED end caps, corner covers, and the NFT channel from Phase C Product 1.3. Those are all injection-scale parts.

Worth asking him: which processes, what maximum platen or shot size, and does he run food-grade material with documented resin traceability — that last one matters for anything touching the crop.
