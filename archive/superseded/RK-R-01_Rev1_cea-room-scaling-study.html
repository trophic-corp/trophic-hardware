01 · Findings

Two things the room reveals that the rack could not

This is what a scaling study is for. Both findings only appear when you multiply the rack by twelve and look at the totals.

Finding 1 — drain-to-waste does not survive scaling

At rack level I recommended draining to waste rather than recirculating, because recirculated nutrient solution moves Pythium from one tray to every tray and a 7–21 day crop gives no time to amortise UV and filtration. That reasoning is still correct at two racks. It stops being affordable at twelve.

ConfigurationConsumed by cropDrained awayWasted per year
2 racks, Ooty R&D, 1 cycle/day24 L/day92 L/day34 m³
9 racks, 1 cycle/day108 L/day414 L/day151 m³
12 racks, 1 cycle/day144 L/day552 L/day201 m³
12 racks, 3 cycles/day432 L/day1,656 L/day604 m³
1,656 litres a day down the drain is not a rounding error, and it is not defensible for a company whose whole positioning is resource efficiency. Only about a fifth of each flood is actually taken up by the media; the rest is perfectly good solution that has touched the crop for ten minutes. A room needs a treated recovery loop — catch tank, filtration, UV, return to reservoir. The UV duty is small: at 40 mJ/cm² the worst case is about 1.15 L/min of continuous treatment, because recovery is spread across the day rather than arriving in a slug.

This does not change the rack. The rack already drains to a header and discharges over an air gap. What changes is what sits under the air gap — a catch tank and treatment skid instead of a floor drain. That is a room-level addition, which is exactly the outcome a scaling study is supposed to produce: a change that lands outside the thing you already designed.

The crossover is roughly four racks. Below it, drain-to-waste with mains top-up is simpler and cheaper. Above it, recovery pays for itself in water alone, before counting the nutrient.

Finding 2 — the floor multiplier is smaller than vertical farming's reputation suggests

LayoutRacksTiersBed areaFloor multiplier
B — serviceable9423.7 m²1.06×
A — max density12431.6 m²1.42×
B, 5-tier9529.6 m²1.33×
A, 5-tier12539.5 m²1.77×

A four-tier rack does not give four times the floor. In a room this small it gives between one and one-and-a-half, because aisles, rack depth and wall clearance consume most of the gain. Nine four-tier racks in 22.3 m² produce 23.7 m² of growing bed — a 1.06× multiplier, essentially the same as growing on the floor. The multiplier is a function of room size, not rack height: aisle area is roughly fixed while bed area grows, so the same racks in a 100 m² room reach 2.5–3×. This is worth knowing before anyone writes it into a business plan, and it is an argument for the 5-tier configuration — which the ergonomics now support, at a lifting index of 0.76.

Why the 150 mm gap is enough. Every rack service is at its ends, not across its back: supply riser and fill manifold on the left upright, drain header on the right, fan end caps at the right of each plenum. Nothing needs access across the full rear face. A 150 mm gap between racks puts every one of those within arm's reach from the aisle — which is only true because the rack was laid out that way in Phase 1, not by luck.
03 · Utilities

Room services

Irrigation

DN32 supply main along the north wall at high level, DN20 branch drops to each rack with a ball valve and union. Pump duty stays 7.2 L/min regardless of rack count because the controller fires one tier at a time across the whole room. Fill window is 72 min per cycle for 9 racks — 8% of a 16 h photoperiod.

Drainage

DN63 main along the south wall at 1:100 fall, taking each rack's DN40 header over its air gap. Terminates at the catch tank, not a floor drain. Peak flow is one tier at 30.7 L/min because draining is sequenced — a hard requirement, not a preference.

Water recovery

Catch tank → 100 µm filter → UV at 40 mJ/cm² → back to reservoir. Duty 0.9–1.2 L/min continuous. Reservoir top-up on level switch. This is the one room-level addition the rack design did not anticipate.

Electrical

3-phase supply, group panel in the plant zone, 3 racks per phase for balance. One 16 A Type A RCBO per rack. Type 2 SPD at the panel, Type 1+2 at the building intake. TN-S earthing to IS 3043, every frame bonded.

HVAC

1.5 TR for 9 racks — 2.46 kW sensible from LEDs and fans, 0.91 kW latent from 32 L/day of transpiration. Ooty's climate means heating and VPD management dominate for much of the year, so specify a reverse-cycle unit rather than cooling-only.

Dehumidification

38 L/day for 9 racks, 51 for 12. Size at 1.2× the transpiration figure. This is the load people under-size: the water the crop does not keep, it exhales.

Take the three-phase supply even at nine racks. 25.4 A single phase is technically possible and operationally awkward — it leaves no headroom for a second dehumidifier, a heater, or the twelfth rack. Three phase costs a little more at connection and makes the room's future somebody else's problem instead of yours.

Forty sensors for a room of nine racks, of which twenty-seven are on the racks. Compare that with what a naive per-rack allocation would produce: a CO₂ sensor, a PAR sensor, pH and EC on every rack would add fifty-four devices that all measure the same thing as their neighbours and all need calibrating. The level allocation is worth roughly ₹1.5 lakh in this room alone, and more in calibration labour every year after.

Automation logic the room owns

  • Irrigation sequencing. The room controller holds one token. Only the rack and tier holding it may fill or drain. That single rule is what keeps the pump at 7.2 L/min and the drain main inside DN63 at any rack count.
  • Photoperiod and dimming per rack, so different crops or stages can run on different racks from one schedule.
  • VPD control computed from room T/RH, driving HVAC and dehumidifier rather than raw humidity — the Phase A requirement.
  • CO₂ dosing to setpoint, interlocked to ventilation and to occupancy. This interlock is a safety function, not a comfort one.
  • Fault containment. A leak or header trip takes one rack out and alarms; the other eight keep running.

Nine of ten properties scale without touching the rack, and the tenth changes something outside it. No rack part, dimension, interface or control behaviour has to be redesigned to build this room. The recovery loop bolts on under an air gap that already exists. That is the result the study was run to find, and it is a good one — but it is worth being clear that it was not luck: sequential firing, end-mounted services and local fail-safe control were each chosen in Phase 1 because of how they would behave at room scale.

Read the split before the total. The racks are 34% of the room. The other two-thirds is envelope, HVAC, water and electrical infrastructure that exists whether you have nine racks or one. That is the honest answer to the earlier pricing anxiety: a customer buying a room is not comparing your rack to a ₹9,999 wire shelf — they are comparing a working, instrumented growing room to building one themselves. It is also the argument for selling rooms rather than racks wherever you can.
What has not been done. No CFD on room air distribution, no thermal model of the envelope against Ooty's actual year, no detailed HVAC selection, and no dashboard or firmware design beyond architecture. The layout, loads and sizing here are closed-form and adequate to commit to a room shape and a service strategy — not to order equipment. Before purchase, the HVAC figure in particular should be checked against a proper year-round load model, because Ooty's heating season is the case that a cooling-led calculation will get wrong.
