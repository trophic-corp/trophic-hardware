Three parts are held and are not in this document: RK-A-301 LED mounting rail, and RK-A-501 / RK-A-502 reflective panels. Overview

The rack

Page 3

A modular four-tier growing rack: galvanised structure, food-grade wet modules, and a 50 mm accessory grid that everything else bolts to.

What it is

Rack A holds sixteen 1020 trays on four tiers at 300, 700, 1100 and 1500 mm, and floods each tier to 22 mm on a ten-minute dwell. The structural set drawn here is 1256 × 563 × 1960 mm and 72.0 kg; the complete rack with irrigation, drainage, lighting, ventilation, sensing and power installed is 1456 × 690 × 1960 mm and 112.7 kg dry.

The design separates three jobs that are usually bundled into one material. The structure carries load and sees humidity but never touches the crop, so it is pre-galvanised steel. The wet module holds the flood and touches the crop, so it alone is food-contact HDPE. The accessory plane — a 50 mm hole grid punched into every upright and beam — carries lighting, sensors, cabling and anything added later. Separating them is what let the frame cost fall by roughly a third without losing the modularity.

The consequence for manufacturing is that almost every part is a cut length of hollow section with punched holes. There is no welding in the rack at all; every structural joint is bolted. That removes pickling and passivation, removes weld distortion from a frame that must hold 3 mm of bed flatness, and means a general fabrication shop can build it.

The one rule that governs every vertical dimension. The top face of the deck mesh panel, RK-A-203, is the bed datum. Deck rail and beam hang below it; tray, canopy and LED are measured up from it. Build each deck to the datum, not to the beam it sits on — the tier section drawing in the Manufacturing Pack shows the full stack.
Two open items carried from validation. The wall anchors RK-A-106 are a fitted item, not an option — an empty rack tips under a 139 N pull at the top bed. And the depth-plane bracing, with per-tier cross beams deleted, is closed only by acceptance test T16, the sway test. Both are recorded in the Engineering Validation Record, RK-A-QC.
Two corrections in this revision are worth reading before you cut anything. The rear X-brace RK-A-104 was dimensioned at 1345 mm in Rev 1 and is 1797 mm — Rev 1 would have produced a part 452 mm too short to fit. And the flood tray RK-A-401 now carries a moulded 30 mm overflow collar and two bosses off the tray centreline; those are form-tool dimensions, not site drilling.
CH 01

RK-A-101 · Upright

Page 4

Carries the whole rack. Also carries the 50 mm accessory grid, which is the interface every other module bolts to.

Description

The upright is the only part in the rack that is genuinely critical, and it is critical for two unrelated reasons. Structurally it is barely working — compressive stress is 2.3 MPa against a 210 MPa yield, and the Euler buckling margin is over a thousand. It is sized at 40 × 40 × 1.6 for dent resistance and for the feel of the product, not for load. What it really does is carry the accessory grid: 36 holes at 50 mm pitch on two adjacent faces, through both walls, in line. Every beam, deck, LED rail, sensor bracket and panel in the system locates off those holes. Get the grid wrong and nothing else in the rack fits.

Manufacturing guidelines

  1. Cut from 6 m stock, four uprights per rack. Cut all four for a given rack from the same bar batch so any mill tolerance is common to the set.
  2. Punch or laser the Ø9 grid. Dimension every hole cumulatively from the foot end, never hole-to-hole — a 0.2 mm incremental error repeated 36 times is 7 mm at the top and the four uprights stop accepting a common beam.
  3. Both faces are punched in one setup where the press allows, to keep the two grids square to each other.
  4. Deburr both sides of every hole. A burr on the inside of the tube is invisible and holds water.
  5. Zinc-rich touch-up on both sawn ends, then press a plastic cap into each end. This is what makes mill-finish GI acceptable in a humid room.
  6. If powder coating: mask the grid holes or ream them after coating. A coated Ø9 will not take an M8 cleanly.

Inspection points

  • Cumulative hole position ±0.2 mm over any 400 mm — check all four uprights, every rack
  • Ø9.0 go / Ø9.3 no-go gauge on a 10% sample of holes
  • Both ends capped and touched up
  • Length 1950 ±1.0 mm
CH 02

RK-A-102 · Beam, long

Page 5

Spans the rack width and carries the deck. Eight at tier level, two in the base frame.

Description

The beam sets the bed flatness, which is the specification that makes flood-and-drain work at all. At the 80 kg design load it deflects 1.79 mm and in normal service 0.50 mm, both inside the 3 mm limit — and that limit exists because a sagging bed gives a different flood depth at the centre trays than at the ends. The section was reduced from 40 × 40 to 30 × 30 during cost reduction after the check showed 40 × 40 was oversized by four times; 30 × 30 × 1.5 still carries a 1.68× margin on the flatness criterion. Bending stress is 37 MPa against 210 MPa yield.

CH 03

RK-A-103 · Beam, short (base)

Page 6

Ties front to rear uprights at the base frame, in the depth plane.

Description

Only two of these, and only at the base. The per-tier cross beams were deliberately deleted during cost reduction on the grounds that each deck assembly already ties front to rear through its own edge frame — building a second tie in steel underneath it was duplication. What remains is this pair at the base plus the rear X-brace, and that combination is the one open structural question in the design. It is scheduled to be closed by acceptance test T16, the depth-plane sway test — 200 N at the top bed, deflection ≤ 10 mm with no permanent set. If P3 fails, the fix is to reinstate per-tier cross beams at about ₹562 a rack; nothing else changes.

CH 04

RK-A-104 · Rear X-brace

Page 7

The only diagonal in the rack. Resists side-sway in both the front and rear planes.

Description

Two of these cross in the rear plane. They exist because deleting the per-tier cross beams left the frame with no diagonal anywhere, and the front plane cannot have one — it has to stay open for tray access. So the rear brace carries side-sway for both planes, through the decks. It costs about ₹210 of flat bar and it is not optional. A rear panel would be a stiffer diaphragm, and the design deliberately does not use it that way: the panel comes off for cleaning, and structure must never depend on a part somebody removes to wash.

Rev 2 correction. Rev 1 dimensioned this part at 1345 mm. Reconciling the drawing against the released model showed the brace spans a 1195 × 1342 mm opening, whose true diagonal is 1797 mm — Rev 1 would have produced a brace 452 mm short, which is not a part that can be fitted at all. The model governs; the length is 1797.

CH 05

RK-A-105 · Levelling foot

Page 8

Levels the rack. Levelness is functional here, not cosmetic.

Description

A purchased item — the drawing is an installation envelope, not a manufacturing drawing. It matters more than a foot usually does, because flood-and-drain needs the beds level: an out-of-level bed gives an uneven flood depth across the four tray positions and the outer trays crop differently from the inner ones. Specify ±20 mm of adjustment so the rack can be levelled on a floor laid to drain, which by definition is not flat. A nylon or PP base rather than bare steel, because it sits on a wet floor.

CH 06

RK-A-106 · Wall anchor bracket

Page 9

Anchors the rack to the wall. Fitted item, not an option.

Description

This part exists because the validation found the rack tips. An empty rack goes over under a 139 N horizontal pull at the top bed — about 14 kg, which anyone yanking a stuck tray will exceed without meaning to. Loaded it takes 307 N. Height-to-depth is 3.48:1, below the 4:1 ratio at which anchoring is normally mandated, so a freestanding rack is defensible on paper; the empty case is not. Two brackets, four bolts, and the problem disappears. If a rack genuinely must stand free, the top tier is to be left unloaded and the rack labelled to say so.

CH 07

RK-A-107 · Beam-end L-bracket

Page 10

Connects beam ends to the upright grid.

Description

The bracket that carries every beam-to-upright joint, twelve per rack. Each one takes a 196 N beam reaction through two M8 bolts, giving a shear safety factor of 157 and a bearing factor of 108 on the 1.6 mm upright wall — enormously over-strength, because the governing constraint is the bolt and hole geometry, not the load. This is also the part that disappears first at volume: a formed keyhole beam-end engaging a slot in the upright deletes all twelve brackets and 48 fasteners, and turns assembly from bolting into dropping a beam in and tapping it down.

CH 08

RK-A-201 · Deck rail, long

Page 11

Front and rear members of the deck frame. Two per tier.

Description

Part of the deck sub-assembly rather than the frame, and the distinction matters: the deck is the constant, and what sits on it — flood table, capillary tray, drained shelf, deep bed, NFT channel, or nothing at all for germination — is the variable. That is what makes one rack serve microgreens, aquatic propagation and germination without redesign. The deck rails also do a structural job the frame no longer does: with the per-tier cross beams deleted, the deck's own edge frame is what ties the front beam to the rear beam.

Page 12

Intermediate deck supports, four per tier, running front to back.

Four per tier, dividing the deck into three bays so the expanded mesh does not sag under a flooded tray. CH 10

RK-A-203 · Deck mesh panel

Page 13

The tray support surface. Its top face is the bed datum.

Description

Straight from commercial greenhouse benching practice, where hot-dip galvanised expanded metal decking is the standard because roughly 76% open area drains freely and passes air without obstructing the tier. The one rule a fabricator must not break is here: the mesh top face is the bed datum. Every vertical dimension in the rack is measured from it — the deck rail and beam hang below it, the tray, canopy and LED are measured up from it. Build the deck to the datum, not to the beam. Rev 2 correction. The model briefly carried this part at a 35% open-area density, which put it at 5.37 kg and the whole rack at 126.2 kg. Expanded mesh at the specified ≥70% open area is 1.98 kg, and the model density has been corrected to 1884 kg/m³ to match. Rack dry mass is 112.7 kg. Where a drawing and the model disagree the model normally governs — this is the one case where the drawing was right.

CH 11

RK-A-401 · Flood tray (wet module)

Page 14

Holds the flood. The only part of the rack the crop touches.

Description

The wet module, and the only structural part in the rack that must be food-contact grade — which is the whole reason the frame does not have to be. Separating the wet zone from the structure is what allowed galvanised steel elsewhere and took roughly a third out of the cost. It floods to 22 mm, holds 14.5 L, and dwells ten minutes; the rim at 47 mm leaves 16.5 L of freeboard, which is what contains a blocked-drain fault long enough for the solenoid to close. Specify it in white: the underside of the tray above then becomes the canopy reflector for the tier below, at no cost.

Rev 2 — three features became tooling dimensions. The overflow is no longer a loose standpipe but a 30 mm collar moulded into the tray floor, which eliminated the failure mode of somebody not refitting it after cleaning. Both penetrations moved off the tray centreline into the rack's wet-services corridor: the drain boss sits 60 mm from the right end and 480 mm from the front edge, the overflow collar 120 mm from the right end on the same centreline, 60 mm apart. Those three numbers are now form-tool geometry, not site drilling, and they must match the rack's drain header spacing across all four trays.

Manufacturing guidelines

  1. Four per rack. Thermoforming is the correct process at 1176 × 560 — tooling is a single aluminium or resin form, roughly ₹30–80k, and it takes the part from about ₹1,228 fabricated to under ₹700.
  2. Injection moulding is not viable at this size for a first production run — it needs a very large press and a tool costing several lakh.
  3. If fabricated from sheet for the prototype: weld corners inside and out, then radius all internal corners so they can be wiped clean.
  4. Ø40 drain boss at 60 mm from the right end and 480 mm from the front edge — on the wet-services centreline, not on the tray centreline. The boss must be the low point: form a fall of 2–3 mm toward it across the tray floor.
  5. Ø32 overflow collar at 120 mm from the right end, same 480 mm centreline, 60 mm from the drain boss. Crest height 30 mm above the tray floor, formed as part of the tool. The crest sets the flood depth mechanically — it is not adjustable and must not be.
  6. Both penetrations take a PP tank connector with the EPDM washer on the wet face. Bore the collar after forming so the crest edge is clean and square.
  7. Flood test every tray to the rim for 30 minutes before it leaves the shop. A leak found in the workshop costs nothing; the same leak found on a loaded rack costs a crop.
  8. Food-contact resin with traceability documentation retained per batch — this is the one material clause that is not negotiable.

Inspection points

  • 30 minute flood test to rim, zero leakage, 100% of trays
  • Fall toward the drain 2–3 mm, no ponding away from the boss
  • Collar crest 30 +0/−1 mm above the tray floor, measured at four points around the crest
  • Boss centres 60 ±1.0 mm apart, and 480 ±1.0 mm from the front edge on all four trays of a rack
  • Internal corners radiused, wipe-clean, no crevice
  • Resin certificate on file per batch
  • Colour white for the reflector function

Conventions

First-angle projection throughout, per ISO and Indian practice — the symbol appears in every title block. Front view top left, end view to its right, plan below, isometric bottom right.

All dimensions in millimetres, unstated. Solid outlines are visible edges; dashed grey is hidden detail such as the inner wall of a hollow section; fine chain lines are centrelines.

Long parts are drawn broken. A zigzag break in the middle of a view means the part is shown shortened so the section stays legible. The overall dimension is always the true length — read the dimension, not the drawing.

Isometric views are illustrative and not to scale. They are there to orient the reader, not to be measured.
